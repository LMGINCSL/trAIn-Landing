Marathon and half

Marathon and half marathon training: what a real week looks like

Most of the kilometres in a marathon plan are easy, and that's no accident. Here are two real weeks, one for a marathon and one for a half, with every session drawn by zone and explained.

Nov 2, 2026 · 4 min read

The rule that keeps coming up: almost everything easy

Studies on how well-trained endurance runners train agree on a pattern: around 80% of sessions are low intensity and the rest are quality. It's known as polarised training, or the 80/20 rule. Running easy most of the time builds your aerobic base without piling up fatigue, and leaves you fresh enough for intervals that count.

In practice, a marathon or half marathon week has four kinds of session:

  • Easy runs (Zone 2): conversational pace. Most of the week.
  • Intervals or surges (Zone 4): short and with recovery, to raise the ceiling.
  • Long run: the key session of the week, also easy.
  • Rest: without it, nothing else gets absorbed.

What these weeks show

Only two quality days. Both weeks have two hard sessions (intervals, fartlek or hills) and the rest is easy. Look at the strip at the top: dark days are few.

Intervals are short and many. 11 × 90 seconds or 7 × 2 minutes, always with easy recovery between. It's easier to hold good effort over short reps than one long one.

The long run is split into blocks. 86 minutes in 15-minute segments with 2 easy minutes between. Time to drink, check how you feel and avoid creeping too fast.

Miss a session and it gets moved, not stacked. The marathon runner skipped the previous week's long run because of a trip. The plan didn't double it: it kept Saturday's long run as it was and added one rep to Monday and Thursday.

A half isn't a scaled-down marathon. Its week is shorter (3 h 45 min vs 5 h 26 min) and swaps intervals for fartlek and trail hills, which work pace and leg strength with less impact.

The last weeks: the taper

Before the race, volume comes down so you arrive fresh. The most cited review on the subject (Bosquet et al., 2007) found the best results with a taper of about two weeks, cutting volume by roughly 40-60% while keeping intensity and frequency. Less volume doesn't mean no fast running: intervals get shorter, but they don't disappear.

Got a race date?

Tell it the race and the day, and the plan spreads easy runs, intervals and long runs up to it, adjusting to how you respond.
